# Routing snapshots

A routing snapshot is the schedule of one service date in a packed form a connection scan reads in the browser: the engine in `assets/js/core/routing_engine.js`, which `assets/js/core/routing_journeys.js` loads (GOB-248). This directory prepares and publishes snapshots and nothing else: no routing algorithm, map integration, offline support, or live routing lives here (GOB-237). Read this before changing the format, the manifest, or how they are published.

## Source and coverage

A snapshot is built from the route schedule view, which holds each route's `schedule-for-route` response for the day before the UTC date, the UTC date, and the day after (GOB-240). One snapshot is built per service date the view holds (decided in GOB-237):

- **Coverage:** the trips of that service date, plus the trips of the previous service date that are still running after the date starts. OneBusAway gives stop times as seconds from the start of the service day, past 86,400 for a trip past midnight, and each schedule names the start of its day in `scheduleDate`, milliseconds since the Unix epoch. A previous-date trip's times move back by the difference of the two starts, 23 or 25 hours on a change of daylight saving time, and only its connections departing at or after the start of the date are kept. A snapshot of the earliest date the view holds has no previous date, which its manifest entry states.
- **Time zone:** the snapshot carries no zone name. `serviceDayStartMs` is the absolute instant of the date's start, from which a reader converts its query time; every agency the responses of 2026-11-07 reference, 11 of them, is `America/Los_Angeles`.
- **Calendar exceptions:** OneBusAway answers each date with the services that run on it, exceptions applied, so every trip a schedule holds runs on its date. Nothing here reads a calendar.
- **Transfers:** derived, because the source has none (`Gobusgo.Routing.Transfers`). A change of trip at one stop takes at least 120 seconds; a walking link joins two stops at most 400 m apart in a straight line, at 1.2 m/s rounded up to the second, and a stop keeps its 64 nearest links. The constants are in the manifest entry's `transferRules` (`minimumTransferS`, `walkRadiusM`, `walkSpeedMPerS`, `walkLinkCountPerStopMax`), and a change to one is a change to the snapshot. The browser applies the same rules to walk from the rider's position to the first stops, reading them from the manifest.
- **Stop positions:** from the stop view, one per stop of the stops file, so the browser can start a journey from the rider's position without sending it anywhere (decided in GOB-241). A stop the stop view does not hold has an unknown position, and no walking link.
- **Display names are in one file of their own, and in no packed file.** The names file holds stop names and codes, route names, colors, and agencies, agency names, and trip headsigns (decided in GOB-251, below). It is the one exception to GOB-237's rule of no display data in a snapshot: offline a page has no view to read names from, and the trip panel, scheduled arrivals, vehicle popups, the summary line, and an agency filter resolved to its routes need them. Keeping them out of the packed files leaves a connection scan reading exactly what it read before. Online, the map's names still come from the views.
- **Map geometry is not in a snapshot.** Route shapes and stop markers come from the route and stop tile archive.

## Format, version 2

`Gobusgo.Routing.SnapshotFormat` writes and reads the files; `assets/js/core/routing_snapshot.js` reads them in the browser. Every number is little-endian. Every file starts with a 16-byte header: the magic `GBRT`, the format version as a `u16`, the kind as a `u16` (1 stops, 2 trips, 3 transfers, 4 connections, 5 positions), the record count as a `u32`, and a `u32` of zero.

| Kind | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Stops | identifier: `u16` byte length, UTF-8 bytes. Index is position, stops in identifier order. |
| Trips | identifier and route identifier, each `u16` length and UTF-8; `u8` service day offset, `0` this date, `1` the previous date. Ordered by offset, then identifier. |
| Transfers | 12 bytes: `u32` from stop, `u32` to stop, `u32` duration in seconds. Ordered by from stop, then to stop. |
| Connections | 24 bytes: `i32` departure and `i32` arrival in seconds from the date's start, `u32` departure stop, `u32` arrival stop, `u32` trip, `u16` sequence within the trip, `u8` flags (bit 0 boarding at the departure stop, bit 1 alighting at the arrival stop), `u8` zero. Ordered by departure, arrival, trip, sequence. |
| Positions | 8 bytes per stop, in the order of the stops file: `i32` latitude and `i32` longitude in millionths of a degree, or `-2147483648`, the `i32` minimum, on both for a stop with no known position. A position outside ±90 or ±180 degrees, or unknown on one axis only, is refused. |

The same trip identifier can appear twice, once per service day offset: they are two runs. A connection's sequence is its hop's position in the whole trip, so a previous-date trip's first kept connection need not have sequence 0.

A position is held in microdegrees as an `i32` because that is a tenth of a meter, three orders of magnitude finer than the 400 m walking radius, and because ±180,000,000 fits an `i32` with room to spare: 8 bytes a stop, about 90 KB for 11,400 stops, where two doubles would take twice that. Whole numbers also encode and decode to the same value in both languages, so the shared fixtures compare them exactly.

Version 2 added the positions file and changed nothing in the other four kinds. Version 1 is not read anywhere: the JavaScript reader refuses it, and the offline bundles a browser saved under it are re-fetched from the manifest, whose paths all changed with the version.

The shared fixtures are in `test/fixtures/routing/v2/`: one file of each kind and `expected.json`, written from `Gobusgo.Test.RoutingSnapshotFixture` by `test/scripts/routing_snapshot_fixtures.exs`. The Elixir tests check that the records encode to the files byte for byte; `node test/scripts/routing_snapshot_reader.mjs` checks that the JavaScript reader decodes them to `expected.json`, and CI does not run it. A change to the format raises the version, writes a directory for the new version, and keeps the old one while a reader of it exists.

## Names file, version 1

`Gobusgo.Routing.Names` builds and writes the names file; `assets/js/names_stream.js` expands it in the browser and `assets/js/core/routing_names.js` reads it. It is one JSON object, encoded with sorted keys and stored as one gzip stream, and it carries its own `version`, 1, apart from the packed format's:

| Field | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| `stopNames`, `stopCodes` | Per stop, in the order of the stops file: its name and its code from the stop view, or `null` where the stop view holds none. |
| `tripHeadsigns` | Per trip, in the order of the trips file: its headsign from its own service date's schedule, or `null` where the schedule has none, so the two runs of one trip identifier each have their own. |
| `routes` | Per route a trip names that the route view holds, by route identifier: `shortName`, `longName`, `color` (six hexadecimal digits), and `agencyId`, each `null` where the source supplies none. `longName` stands in for a missing short name, as on the map. |
| `agencyNames` | Per agency those routes name that the agency view holds, by agency identifier: its name. |

Stops and trips are keyed by index because the connections already address them by index, so a column needs no identifier and a reader takes a stop's name from the index it already holds; a names file is therefore read only with the stops and trips files of its own manifest entry, and the reader refuses columns of other lengths than theirs. Routes and agencies have no index in a snapshot and are keyed by identifier.

JSON gzipped was chosen over a packed kind by size and by what the browser needs (decided in GOB-270). The names repeat heavily, a few hundred headsigns across thousands of trips, and gzip removes the repetition, while the routing files are served as they are stored, with no content encoding, so a packed kind would travel uncompressed. A packed kind would also be a change to the packed format and raise its version, which a deployed page refuses. The browser expands the stream with `DecompressionStream`, at most 16 MiB, and parses it with `JSON.parse`; the reader is otherwise pure.

Measured on 2026-11-08 over the stored responses of 2026-11-08, which cover two agencies: the snapshot of 2026-11-08, 5,837 stops, 9,122 trips with 223 distinct headsigns, 113 named routes, and 316,851 connections, has a names file of 54 KB, 518 KB expanded. Gzipped alone, its stop names take 29 KB, stop codes 13 KB, headsigns 8 KB, and routes 1 KB. A packed kind of the same names, with the headsigns in a table, is estimated from the expanded columns at about 215 KB. Scaled to the 11,433 stops and the 493,420 connections of a date built from the 272 schedules of 2026-11-07, the full network's file is estimated at about 110 KB, 900 KB expanded; that estimate is not measured.

The shared fixture is in `test/fixtures/routing/names-v1/`: `names.json.gz` and `expected.json`, written from `Gobusgo.Test.RoutingNamesFixture` by `test/scripts/routing_snapshot_fixtures.exs`. The Elixir tests check that the names encode to the stream's content byte for byte and decode to `expected.json`; `node test/scripts/routing_names_reader.mjs` checks that the JavaScript reader expands and decodes it to `expected.json`, and CI does not run it. The stream itself is compared by content, never by bytes, since another zlib can compress the same content to other bytes. A change to the names file raises its `version` and writes a directory for the new version; the packed format's version stays as it is.

## Trip paths file, version 1

A date's snapshot has trip path files beside it: the path of every trip that `Gobusgo.OneBusAway.TripPath` can build, in a form a page decodes a trip at a time (GOB-357). They are kinds 1 and 2 of the packed container (`lib/gobusgo/packed/AGENTS.md`), as GOB-369 decided after the issue text asked for gzipped JSON. A shard is one route's trips on one date, and the index names the date's shards.

A geometry is a distinct sequence of points, a pattern is a geometry with the distance of each stop along it, and a trip is a pattern with its own stop times. A route's trips are grouped by stop sequence, one path is built per sequence with `TripPath.build_route`, in batches of 100, and every trip of the sequence shares it. Two sequences with one polyline share a geometry, and two that also stand at the same distances share a pattern. 906 sequences of 2026-11-09 made 897 geometries.

**Kind 1, trip path shard, version 1** (`Gobusgo.Routing.TripPathShard`, `assets/js/core/trip_path_shard.js`). One record per trip, ordered by trip identifier; the strings are the trip identifiers. The shard names no route and no service date, so one schedule and route path give the same bytes on any date. Four sections follow the string table:

| Section | Holds |
| --- | --- |
| 1 geometries | a varint count, then each geometry as a varint count of points and per point the zigzag varint latitude and longitude steps from the point before, the first from (0, 0), in 1e-5 degrees: the encoded polyline's own integers |
| 2 patterns | a varint count, then per pattern the varint geometry index, the varint stop count, and per stop the zigzag varint distance step in meters from the stop before, the first from 0 |
| 3 trips | one `u32` column of three blocks of one value per trip: the trip identifier's string index, the pattern index, and the byte offset of the trip's times in section 4 |
| 4 times | per trip, per stop of its pattern, the zigzag varint arrival step in seconds from the stop before's arrival, the first from 0, and the zigzag varint dwell, departure minus arrival |

A trip with no path, one a stop of which has no position in the stop view, is left out; a route whose trips have none gets no shard. A reader refuses a geometry of no point, a pattern of no stop or with a step back, a string, pattern, or time offset the file does not hold, a trip identifier used twice, and times that do not lie one after another to the section's end. The JavaScript reader checks the whole file when it decodes it and decodes a trip's points and stops only when `tripPathFromShard` asks, into the frozen object `decodeTripPath` returns, through the one constructor `tripPathOf` of `assets/js/core/trip_path.js`. It builds one Map from trip identifier to record the first time a trip is asked for, bounded by the shard's trips, where a search of the sorted identifiers would depend on a string order that UTF-16 and UTF-8 do not share.

**Kind 2, trip path index, version 1** (`Gobusgo.Routing.TripPathIndex`). One record per shard, ordered by route identifier; the strings are the route identifiers and the shard paths. Section 1 is one `u32` column of three blocks, the route's string index, the path's string index, and the shard's `byteSize`, and section 2 is each shard's SHA-256 as 32 raw bytes. A record is the shard's manifest entry: `path`, `byteSize`, and `sha256` of the gzip stream.

Each file is stored through `Gobusgo.Writer` at `routing/snapshots/dt=<build date>/trip-paths-<sha256>.bin.gz` and `trip-paths-index-<sha256>.bin.gz`, hashed as `Gobusgo.Packed.stored/1` does, and the entry's `files.tripPaths` is the index's `path`, `byteSize`, and `sha256`. `formatVersion` stays 2 and the entry gained a key a deployed page ignores. Shards and index are written before the entry is recorded, so the manifest names no missing file. A date whose routes have no path has no `files.tripPaths`.

`Gobusgo.Routing.Snapshots.TripPaths` builds them after the date's snapshot files are written and its process has ended, in a process of its own, linked, with `fullsweep_after: 0` and a heap cap of 8M words (`:trip_path_heap_words_max`). It reads the views one route at a time, writes each shard as it is built, and returns the index records. A process that outgrows the cap is killed and logged as `Routing trip paths build killed` at `error`; one that exits another way or whose write fails is logged as `Routing trip paths build failed`. Either way the entry is recorded without `files.tripPaths` and the snapshot stays, and only the trip paths are built again (GOB-373): `Gobusgo.Routing.TripPathRetry`, which is pure, gives the schedule, 10 minutes after the failure, then 30, then hourly, counted from the last failure, and the run that finds the date due builds its trip paths again, in the same process and under the same cap, from the views as they are, leaving the files its entry names as built, and records `files.tripPaths` once they are written. The failure count and time are held with the entry in `Gobusgo.Routing.Snapshots`, so a build of changed inputs, which records a new entry, and a build on a later day start the count again, and a restart, which empties the process, builds the date from scratch at once. Each failure log carries `attempt`, and the success after a failure logs `Routing trip paths built after failure` at `info` with the attempt. The inputs hash includes the route path view's request identifiers of the routes the date's schedules name, so a polyline fetched again rebuilds the date. `Routing snapshots built` carries `trip_path_count`, `trip_path_shard_count`, `trip_path_elapsed_ms`, `trip_path_failed_count`, and `trip_path_retried_count`, the dates whose trip paths alone were built again.

Measured on 2026-11-10 over the stored responses of 2026-11-07 to 2026-11-10 (GOB-355): the date of 2026-11-09 has 380 shards of 22,242 trips, 1.21 MB of gzip in all, a median of 3.05 KB and a most of 10.3 KB, and an index of 32.3 KB, against 1.24 MB, 3.1 KB, 10.7 KB, and 32 KB for the same paths as gzipped JSON. Geometries are in 1e-5 degrees, the source's precision, where the core's positions are microdegrees: every coordinate is a multiple of ten microdegrees, so the finer unit only added about 3.3 bits to every step, and the same shards with microdegree steps took 1.40 MB. Writing each trip's times as its arrival steps and then its dwells saved 1.1% and all trips' arrival steps and then all dwells 1.5%, under the 5% asked for, so the times stay in stop order. A date's shards take 2.8 to 3.1 s to build on a development machine, 1.75 s of it `TripPath.build_route`, and the process's heap peaked at 1.49M words with the microdegree steps; a cap of 3M words kills it and 4M passes. The shards of both dates equal `TripPath.build_route` over all 44,487 trips, points, distances, and times, with 0 mismatches, and 44,483 of them pass `preparePath`, the four Kitsap trips of one-point paths excepted.

The vehicle page reads the shards (GOB-358): `assets/js/core/trip_path_files.js` takes the manifest entry whose `serviceDayStartMs` is a vehicle's service date, fetches that entry's index and the shard of the trip's route, and puts the trip's path in the page's cache. Measured on 2026-11-10 against the server on localhost over the stored responses of 2026-11-07 to 2026-11-10, a downtown view at zoom 15 fetched the manifest, one index of 31.5 KB, and 39 shards of 171.5 KB in all over 43 s as vehicles came into view, and found no path for two trips of a route with none. The offline bundle holds each saved date's trip path index and every shard the index names, at most 1,000, so the page reads the same `/routing/` addresses offline as online (GOB-360). `offline_schedules.js` stores the index with the date's other files, reads it with `decodeTripPathIndex`, and stores the shards one at a time, each only when it holds the `byteSize` the manifest gives; a date with no `files.tripPaths` saves without them. The bundle's own manifest keeps `files.tripPaths`, and drops it for a date saved without its trip paths. The Trip tab reads the same shards for the rides of the chosen journey (GOB-466): `assets/js/trip_path_reader.js` runs the plans of `trip_path_files.js` with the fetchers of `assets/js/trip_path_fetch.js`, which the vehicle page's markers use too, so the browser's cache serves a shard the other fetched first. A ride leg carries `boardSequence` and `alightSequence`, the sequences of the connections it boards and leaves at, and a connection's sequence is its hop's position in the whole trip, so the ride takes the pattern's stops `boardSequence` through `alightSequence + 1`; `assets/js/core/ride_path.js` cuts the geometry between those stops' distances, with the two cut points interpolated. A run of the previous date reads its shard under that date's service day. The ride keeps the line through its stops, with the reason named in the result, where the leg's stop count is not the pattern's hops, the pattern is shorter than the leg, the two stops stand at one distance or past the path's end by more than 1 m, the path has one point, or a stop has no position or stands more than 200 m from the point its distance cuts. Over a one-date sample (2026-11-11 shards, a 2026-11-13 snapshot, stop positions from stored route paths), none of the 7,762 rides cut fell back for a detached stop or a mismatched pattern.

The shared fixtures are in `test/fixtures/routing/trip-paths-v1/`: `shard.bin`, the loop route 1_7994's shard, `index.bin`, an index of two hand-written records, each also as a gzip stream, and `expected.json`, written from `Gobusgo.Test.TripPathShardFixture` by `test/scripts/trip_path_fixtures.exs`. `node test/scripts/trip_path_shard_check.mjs` decodes them and checks that the loop trip read from the shard deep-equals `decodeTripPath` of the encoded event.


## Files and manifest

`Gobusgo.Routing.Snapshot` splits a date's connections into chunks by the hour of their departure, about 30 a day, and gives the stop, trip, transfer, and position tables files of their own, and the names a file of its own (decided in GOB-237 and GOB-251). A reader fetches the chunks from the hour of its query onward and stops once a chunk departs after the cutoff, the best arrival it has found plus a window; the journeys that arrive by the cutoff are those reading the whole snapshot gives (decided in GOB-241, below). Each file is stored through `Gobusgo.Writer` at `routing/snapshots/dt=<build date>/<kind>-<sha256>.bin`, and the names file at `routing/snapshots/dt=<build date>/names-<sha256>.json.gz`, which the writer stores compressed. The names file's hash and `byteSize` are those of the gzip stream, the bytes a reader fetches; the build computes the stream with the same `Gobusgo.Compression.compress/1` the writer calls, so the two agree.

`GET /api/routing/manifest` answers the manifest from `Gobusgo.Routing.Snapshots`; its response is described by `components.schemas.RoutingManifest` in `priv/openapi.json`, and it holds one entry per service date, whose `files` name the files this section stores.

The names file left `formatVersion` at 2: no packed file changed, and the entry gained one key under `files`, which a deployed page ignores, since `routing_journeys.js` fetches the four tables by name and the offline bundle of a page loaded before the change saves the files it names.

The files are served under `/routing`: locally from `<GOBUSGO_DATA_DIR>/routing` by a `Plug.Static` plug, and with a bucket from the bucket through CloudFront, both cacheable for a year as immutable, since a file's address never holds other content. Binary payloads never travel over the LiveView socket; a page may hand a reader the manifest's address.

## Routing in the browser

`assets/js/core/routing_journeys.js` decides a query from the files alone (GOB-248), `assets/js/routing_driver.js` fetches the files it asks for (GOB-420), and the Trip tab of the vehicle page routes with it, fetching the files under `/routing/` (GOB-241). The trip planner app of the MCP endpoint (GOB-388) routes with it too, through the same `assets/js/trip_route.js`, from a frame of another origin: `fetchFile` of that module puts the origin the document names in front of each path the manifest names, and `/api/routing/manifest` answers a page of any origin with `access-control-allow-origin: *`, so the manifest and the files, which CloudFront serves, are read from the public host. Its rules, decided in GOB-241:

- **Fetching.** Every file goes through a function the caller hands in, which receives a path the manifest names and nothing else, so the rider's position never leaves the browser. The core fetches nothing: `startQuery` and `resumeQuery` return the files a query needs next, and the driver fetches them through that function, holds their bytes for the query, and hands every held file to the next resume, at most `ROUND_COUNT_MAX` times (GOB-420). Each fetch has 20 seconds, a file of another size than the manifest's `byteSize` is refused, and a manifest of another `formatVersion` or with more than 48 chunks is refused before anything is fetched. A refusal or a failed fetch is an outcome naming the file, never a throw.
- **Loading by window.** It asks for the four tables together, then the chunks from the one holding its departure onward, one at a time, routing over every held chunk after each. The core keeps no bytes between rounds, so each round decodes the tables and the held chunks again. It stops before the first chunk whose `departureSMin` is past the cutoff, the earliest arrival found plus `WINDOW_SECONDS`, 45 minutes, and returns the frontier journeys arriving by the cutoff, fewest changes first, with the earliest marked. A ride names every stop it passes, the trip's connections from its boarding connection to its alighting one, and a journey holds the position of each stop it names whose position the positions file knows, so a page draws a journey with no request. The scan reads connections from the departure onward and stops at the best arrival, so a journey arriving by the cutoff uses only connections departing before it, which the loaded chunks hold: the result is the whole snapshot's frontier cut at the cutoff, and `node test/scripts/routing_journeys_check.mjs` checks that over a truncated chunk set. A destination no journey reaches reads every chunk from the departure on.
- **A start from a position.** `assets/js/core/routing_walk.js` takes the stops within `walkRadiusM` of the position in a straight line, at most `walkLinkCountPerStopMax` of them, nearest first, each with its distance at `walkSpeedMPerS` rounded up to the second, from the positions file and the manifest's `transferRules`. The scan starts from each with its walk, and takes no walking link from them.
- **A destination at a position** (GOB-375). The router routes to one stop, so the Trip tab routes to a place, an address, or an intersection outside it: `targetStops` of `assets/js/core/point_journeys.js` takes the stops within a walk of the point by the same rules, the nearest 4, the tab routes to each, arriving there by the time less the stop's walk for Arrive by, and `journeysToPoint` adds the walk from each journey's stop as its last step and joins the answers in one frontier, the earliest arrival, or the latest departure for Arrive by, cut by the same window. Neither routing module changes.
- **A ride's walk.** A walking link follows a ride whenever the ride reaches its stop earlier than any ride before it in the round's labels, whatever reached the stop on foot, so an earlier walk to a stop never keeps a later ride from walking on from it (GOB-376). Replayed over 500 random stop-to-stop queries of the snapshot of 2026-11-10, the rule changed 20 frontiers from the one that gated the walk on the stop's arrival of any kind, and found an earlier arrival for 11, a later one for none.
- **The service date of a departure.** `serviceDateAt` picks the latest snapshot whose `serviceDayStartMs` is at or before the departure, while a chunk departs at or after it. Past midnight that is the new date, whose snapshot holds the previous date's trips still running, so every trip running at the departure is in it. Before midnight it is the date then running, whose trips past midnight it holds. A departure past a snapshot's last connection, with no later snapshot started, has no service date.
- **Into the next service date** (decided in GOB-252). Where the chosen date has no journey, or its earliest arrival comes after the date's last connection departs, `routeJourneys` goes on into the snapshot whose `serviceDayStartMs` is next in the manifest, so a late query finds the first morning trips. The departure moves into that date's seconds, before its start and so negative, and the scan begins at its first connection; the trips of the chosen date past midnight are in both snapshots, and of two journeys with the same arrival and changes the frontier keeps the chosen date's. The two frontiers meet on one timeline: a journey is kept when it arrives strictly earlier than every one with no more changes, the window counts from the earliest arrival of either date, the next date's chunks load by that window, and each journey carries the `serviceDate` its times count from. It goes one date further and never more, each snapshot keeps the 48-chunk limit, and with no later snapshot, or a stop or a start the next date's files lack, the answer is the chosen date's.
- **Arriving by a time** (GOB-376). `routeArrivingBy` in the engine is a reverse connection scan over the same columns: round k holds, per stop, the latest time a rider can leave it, the latest a vehicle can arrive at it, and the latest a ride boards there, with at most k rides still to take. It reads the connections from the last one departing at or before the arrival down to the first, alights a trip at the latest of its stops it can leave by then, and boards it at every earlier stop. One walk may reach the target, one may come before each ride whenever that ride boards later than any ride before at its stop, and a start stop from a position is left only by boarding, the walks of a forward query run backward. A round's journey joins the frontier when it leaves strictly later than the round before, and it stops at the first connection departing at or before the best departure. For every journey of the shared fixtures, a forward query from its departure arrives by the time and one from a second later does not, and so it did for 566 random queries of the snapshot of 2026-11-10, half from a stop and half from the walk starts around one.
- **Loading backward.** `routeJourneysArrivingBy` loads the four tables, then the chunks from the last one whose `departureSMin` is at or before the arrival down to the first, routing after each, and stops before the first chunk whose `departureSMax` is before the cutoff, the latest departure found less `WINDOW_SECONDS`. It returns the frontier journeys that leave by the cutoff or later, fewest changes first, the latest marked with `isLatest`, each with its `departureS`. A journey that leaves by the cutoff uses only connections departing at or after it, which the loaded chunks hold, so the answer is the whole snapshot's frontier cut at the cutoff; `node test/scripts/routing_journeys_check.mjs` checks that over a truncated chunk set, and the replay above checked it on the 283 queries from a stop. An arrival the network cannot meet reads every chunk from the arrival back.
- **Into the previous service date.** The arrival belongs to the service date `serviceDateAt` gives it. Where that date has no journey, or its latest leaves before the date's first connection departs, the query goes back into the snapshot whose `serviceDayStartMs` comes before, with the arrival moved into that date's seconds, so an arrival soon after midnight finds the trips that left before it, which the later snapshot does not hold. The frontiers meet on one timeline as they do going forward: a journey is kept when it leaves strictly later than every one with no more changes, the window counts back from the latest departure of either date, and of two journeys with the same departure and changes the frontier keeps the chosen date's. It goes one date back and never more.

Measured on 2026-11-08 in node 24.21.0 on a development machine, over the snapshot of 2026-11-08 built from the 249 schedules stored that day: 4,801 stops, 258,714 connections in 30 chunks, 6.2 MB of chunks and 633 KB of tables, the positions file 38 KB. A stop-to-stop query that found a journey fetched 3 to 5 chunks, 1.1 to 2.3 MB with the tables, and answered in 7 to 30 ms from files on disk; a query whose destination no journey reached fetched every chunk after its departure, up to 4.6 MB, in up to 191 ms. A start from a position found its journey with 4 chunks, 2.0 MB, in 14 ms, and one whose destination no journey reached read 22 chunks, 5.9 MB, in 261 ms. ## Publication

`Gobusgo.Routing.Snapshots.Build` runs every ten minutes wherever the sources are scheduled. For each held date it hashes the inputs, the request identifiers of the date's and the previous date's schedules, the stop view's positions and names, the route view's route names, and the agency view's agency names, the names as values so a view fetched again unchanged builds nothing, and passes over a date whose hash is that of its current entry built today. Otherwise it builds, writes every file, and only then records the entry, so the manifest never names a file that is not stored. A build on a later day writes the files again under that day, which keeps every named file inside the two days of `Gobusgo.RetentionPolicy` and the bucket's lifecycle rule. Vehicle positions never trigger a build: only a schedule fetch, a stop position, or a changed name changes the inputs. Each date is built in a linked process of its own, spawned with `fullsweep_after: 0` and a heap cap of 20M words (`:date_heap_words_max`), and its trip path files in another once that process has ended (see "Trip paths file"); a date whose build outgrows the cap is killed, logged as `Routing snapshot build killed` at `error`, and counted as failed, and the dates after it are still built (GOB-274). The connections stay packed from the compact schedules' binaries to the files: `Gobusgo.Routing.Timetable` writes each connection as a 24-byte key that sorts as a binary in connection order, into one binary per hour of departure, sorts each hour's keys alone, and turns them into the records of the connections file, and `Gobusgo.Routing.Snapshot` cuts the chunks out of the records (GOB-275, GOB-279). The date's process reads the view one route at a time and holds each schedule packed into a binary, off its heap, and every pass of the timetable and the headsigns unpacks one schedule at a time from those binaries, so no pass holds a list of a date's schedules or connections, and a fetch the view takes in during a build changes nothing the build reads (GOB-279). Replayed on 2026-11-09 against production's stored responses of 2026-11-07 to 2026-11-09, two runs per cap, the date of 676,857 connections needs a cap between 12M and 14M words, where one sort over the whole date needed between 42M and 45M and a map per hop and per connection between 80M and 90M; the date's process peaks at a heap of 47 MB, where it peaked at 168 MB, and the files are byte for byte the same. The release runs with the VM's default segment cache, which keeps up to 10 freed segments per allocator instance resident: under the container's 1,536 MB limit on two schedulers, a start replayed against the same responses peaks at 678 to 841 MiB of the container's anonymous memory over 15 runs, where it peaked at 1,397 MiB or was killed while the date's heap reached 168 MB (GOB-279). The names reach each date's process as one packed binary, which is stored off the process heap, and are decoded only once the timetable is built, so the timetable's peak shares the cap with none of the names (GOB-270). Every file but the names file is byte for byte the one built without it, and the caps measured above are those of a build that writes the names file.

The manifest is held in the process and starts empty on every start; the first build after a start, at the scheduler's first run, builds every date. Nothing is cached in memory beyond that manifest, and no store other than the writer's is used.

Measured on 2026-11-08 with the 272 live schedules of 2026-11-07 standing in for three dates: a build of the three took 2.6 s on a development machine and a check that built nothing 42 ms. A date with its previous date held 493,420 connections in 30 chunks, 11.8 MB of connection files and 5.0 MB gzipped, with a 97 KB stop table and a 480 KB trip table.

## Live-routing overlay

Live data is a later overlay on top of a snapshot, and none of it is built yet. A snapshot never changes: delays, cancellations, and revised stop times live in the overlay; vehicle positions stay on the map's update path and never reach routing data.

- **Compatibility.** An overlay names the snapshot it applies to by `serviceDate` and the SHA-256 of its trips file, since it addresses trips by index; an overlay for any other snapshot is ignored.
- **Freshness and expiry.** An overlay carries the time it was built and an expiry. A reader applies an overlay only before its expiry, and once it expires or is missing the reader routes on the scheduled times alone, which is always a valid answer.
- **Ordering.** A connection scan needs connections in departure order. Applying a delay can move a connection past others, so an overlay is applied by producing a re-ordered set of connections, never by changing times in place.
- **Transfer feasibility.** A delayed arrival can make a planned transfer miss its connection, and an earlier one can make a missed transfer possible. The scan's minimum transfer time and walking links apply to the live times, so feasibility is decided after the overlay is applied.
